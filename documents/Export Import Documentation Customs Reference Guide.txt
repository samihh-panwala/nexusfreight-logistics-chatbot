1. Why Documentation Matters
Every international shipment is, in a very real sense, a paper trail before it is a physical movement of goods. Customs
authorities, banks, carriers, and insurers never see the cargo itself — they see the documents that describe it, and
they release, finance, insure, or clear the shipment based on whether those documents are complete, consistent with
one another, and accurate. A shipment can be physically ready to move and still sit at a port or airport for days
because a single figure on an invoice does not match the corresponding figure on the packing list, or because a
certificate that a destination country requires was never obtained. Understanding documentation is therefore not a
peripheral compliance detail; it is the mechanism that determines how quickly and how cheaply a shipment actually
moves.
It is useful to separate the export side of a transaction from the import side, since the two involve different authorities,
different documents, and different points of responsibility, even though they describe the same physical shipment.
The exporter's documentation exists to satisfy the customs authority in the country of origin that the goods are legally
permitted to leave, to give the carrier what it needs to accept and transport the cargo, and to give the buyer proof of
what was shipped. The importer's documentation exists to satisfy the customs authority in the destination country that
duties and taxes have been correctly declared and paid, and that the goods are permitted to enter. A single shipment
will typically generate both an export-side document set, prepared largely by or on behalf of the seller, and an
import-side document set, prepared largely by or on behalf of the buyer, with a handful of documents such as the
commercial invoice and bill of lading serving both purposes at once.

3. Bill of Lading
The bill of lading is arguably the single most important document in ocean freight, because it performs three distinct
functions simultaneously rather than just one. First, it is a receipt issued by the carrier acknowledging that specific
cargo, in a stated condition and quantity, has been received for shipment. Second, it is evidence of the contract of
carriage between the shipper and the carrier, setting out the terms under which the cargo will be transported. Third —
and this is the function that makes it unusual among shipping documents — a bill of lading issued in negotiable form
is itself a document of title, meaning whoever legitimately holds the original bill of lading has the legal right to claim
the goods at destination. This third function is why banks financing a trade transaction under a letter of credit insist on
holding the original bill of lading until payment conditions are met: possession of the document controls possession of
the cargo.
In practice, three variants of the bill of lading come up most often. A negotiable (or 'order') bill of lading can be
transferred to a new holder by endorsement, which is what makes it usable as loan collateral and as the
payment-control mechanism in letter-of-credit transactions. A straight bill of lading names a specific consignee and
cannot be transferred to anyone else, which simplifies matters when payment has already been settled outside the
documentary process and there is no need for the document to change hands. A seaway bill dispenses with the
document-of-title function altogether: the consignee named on it can claim the goods by proving identity, without
needing to present a physical original, which speeds up release at destination for shipments between trusted trading
partners where collateral control is unnecessary. Allcargo, acting as an NVOCC through ECU Worldwide, issues its
own house bills of lading to shippers even when the underlying ocean transport is performed under a separate carrier
(master) bill of lading between ECU Worldwide and the vessel operator.
4. Commercial Invoice, Packing List, and Certificate of Origin
Commercial Invoice
The commercial invoice is the exporter's formal bill for the goods, and it is the document customs authorities in the
destination country rely on most heavily when assessing duties and taxes, since it states the transaction value, the
agreed terms of sale, and the identities of the buyer and seller. Because duty is typically calculated as a function of
declared value, any inconsistency between the commercial invoice and other documents in the set — most
commonly the packing list or the bill of lading — is one of the most frequent triggers for a customs hold, covered
further in Section 11.
Packing List
Where the commercial invoice states value, the packing list states physical detail: the number of packages, what is
inside each one, and the weight and dimensions of each. Customs officers and warehouse staff use the packing list
to physically verify a shipment against its paperwork without needing to open every package, which is why an
accurate, itemized packing list can meaningfully speed up a physical inspection if one is triggered, while a vague one
makes an inspection slower and more likely to raise further questions.
Certificate of Origin
A certificate of origin formally states the country in which the goods were manufactured or produced, which matters
for two separate reasons that are easy to conflate. The first is straightforward customs compliance: many countries
simply require origin to be declared regardless of any trade agreement. The second is preferential tariff treatment:
where the origin country has a free trade agreement with the destination country, a properly issued certificate of origin

rules of origin that the relevant trade agreement requires.
5. Letter of Credit
A letter of credit is a payment mechanism, issued by the buyer's bank at the buyer's request, under which the bank
commits to pay the seller once the seller presents a specified set of documents that comply exactly with the terms the
letter of credit lays out. Its purpose is to substitute the bank's creditworthiness for the buyer's, which is particularly
valuable in international trade where the seller may have no practical way to assess an unfamiliar overseas buyer's
ability or willingness to pay, and no straightforward legal recourse if the buyer simply does not.
The mechanism only works because the bank's payment obligation is triggered purely by documents, not by whether
the underlying goods actually arrived in good condition or at all. This is the single most important thing to understand
about how a letter of credit functions in practice: the issuing bank examines the documents the seller presents —
typically the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, and insurance certificate, in whatever
combination the letter of credit specifies — and pays if those documents match the letter of credit's requirements
exactly. Even a minor discrepancy, such as a shipment date one day later than the letter of credit permits, or a
misspelled company name, gives the bank grounds to refuse payment until the discrepancy is corrected or the buyer
specifically waives it. This is why exporters shipping under a letter of credit need every document in the set to be
internally consistent and to match the letter of credit's stated terms precisely, and why document preparation errors
are treated as a serious commercial risk rather than a minor administrative inconvenience in letter-of-credit
transactions specifically.
6. Shipping Bill, Bill of Entry, and Insurance Certificate
Shipping Bill
The shipping bill is the export declaration filed with customs in the country of origin, and cargo cannot legally leave
the country until it has been filed and cleared. It declares what is being exported, its value, and the exporter's claim to
any export incentive schemes the origin country may offer. In India, for example, the shipping bill is filed
electronically, and its clearance is a prerequisite for the goods to be loaded onto the vessel or aircraft.
Bill of Entry
The bill of entry is the mirror-image document on the import side: it is filed by the importer, typically through a customs
broker, to declare goods arriving into the destination country and to calculate the duties and taxes owed before the
goods can be released from customs custody. Three sub-types matter in practice. A bill of entry for home
consumption is used when the importer wants the goods released immediately for domestic use, with duty paid up
front. A bill of entry for warehousing is used when the importer wants the goods moved into a bonded warehouse
without paying duty immediately, deferring that payment until the goods are later cleared for domestic use, discussed
further in Section 9. An ex-bond bill of entry is then filed at that later point to clear goods out of bonded storage into
the domestic market.
Insurance Certificate
The insurance certificate evidences that the cargo is covered against loss or damage during transit, and who is
responsible for arranging it is determined by the Incoterm governing the sale rather than by any fixed rule — under
some terms the seller must insure the goods on the buyer's behalf, under others the buyer arranges its own coverage
once risk transfers. Where a letter of credit is in use, the insurance certificate is often a required presentation
document, and its coverage amount and named risks typically need to match the letter of credit's specific

7. HS Codes and Tariff Classification
The Harmonized System, commonly abbreviated HS, is an internationally standardized numerical classification
system for traded goods, maintained by the World Customs Organization and used, in some form, by essentially
every customs authority in the world. Every product moving across a border is assigned an HS code, and that code is
what determines the duty rate applied, whether the product requires any special permit or license, and whether it
qualifies for preferential treatment under a trade agreement. Getting the HS code wrong is one of the more
consequential documentation errors a shipper can make, because it can result in the wrong duty being paid, a
shipment being held for reclassification, or in more serious cases, penalties for misdeclaration.
The system is structured hierarchically. The first six digits of an HS code are standardized globally under the World
Customs Organization's system, meaning a six-digit HS code identifies the same general product category anywhere
in the world. Individual countries then add further digits — commonly extending the code to eight or ten digits — to
create more granular national tariff lines specific to their own duty schedules. This means two countries may agree on
the first six digits of a product's classification while differing on the final digits, and an exporter shipping the same
product to multiple destination countries may need a different full HS code for each one even though the underlying
product has not changed.
Because classification can be genuinely ambiguous for some products — a product that combines materials or
functions from more than one HS chapter is a common source of disagreement — many customs authorities offer a
binding ruling process. An importer or exporter can apply in advance for a binding ruling that fixes the correct HS
code for a specific product, and once issued, that ruling is legally binding on the customs authority for future
shipments of the same product. For a shipper who moves the same product repeatedly and wants certainty over duty
treatment, obtaining a binding ruling once is generally a far more reliable approach than relying on a broker's
classification judgment shipment after shipment, since it removes the risk of a customs officer reclassifying the
product differently at a later date.

The green-versus-red channel distinction is worth understanding in more depth because it is the single biggest factor
in how quickly a given shipment clears. Channel assignment is generally automated and driven by a risk-scoring
algorithm rather than a manual decision, which means an importer's own track record of accurate, consistent filings
over time genuinely reduces the likelihood of future shipments being flagged, while a history of discrepancies
increases it. This is one of the more practical reasons documentation accuracy matters beyond any single shipment:
it shapes the risk profile customs assigns to an importer going forward.
9. Bonded vs. Open Warehousing
The distinction between bonded and open warehousing comes down to whether import duty has been paid on the
goods sitting inside the warehouse. A bonded warehouse is a facility licensed by customs authorities to store
imported goods before duty has been paid, under a form of ongoing customs supervision and financial guarantee (the
'bond' the term refers to). Goods can sit in bonded storage for an extended period without triggering a duty obligation,
and duty only becomes payable at the point the goods are actually withdrawn for domestic use, using the ex-bond bill
of entry described in Section 6. If the goods are instead re-exported directly from bonded storage without ever
entering the domestic market, import duty is never triggered at all.
An open (or 'free') warehouse, by contrast, stores goods on which duty has already been paid and cleared, meaning
the goods are, from a customs perspective, no different from any other domestic inventory sitting in storage. The
practical choice between the two comes down to cash-flow timing and the certainty of the goods' final destination: an
importer who is uncertain whether goods will ultimately be sold domestically or re-exported, or who simply wants to
defer the cash outlay of duty payment until closer to the point of sale, benefits from bonded storage, while an importer
who already knows the goods are destined for the domestic market and wants to avoid the administrative overhead of
bonded-goods tracking is often better served by open warehousing once clearance is complete.
10. Demurrage and Detention
Demurrage and detention are both charges levied by a carrier or terminal for the extended use of shipping equipment
beyond a free period, but they apply to different things and it is a common point of confusion to treat them as
interchangeable. Demurrage applies to a container while it remains within the port or terminal premises beyond its
free time — in effect, rent charged for the container occupying valuable terminal space instead of being moved out
promptly after discharge. Detention applies to the container once it has left the terminal and is in the consignee's
possession for unloading, and it is charged for the time the container itself is held outside the terminal beyond its free
period, before being returned empty to the carrier.
Both charges exist for the same underlying reason: shipping lines own a finite number of containers, and every day a
container sits idle — whether stuck at a terminal awaiting customs clearance or sitting at a consignee's warehouse
awaiting unloading — is a day that container is unavailable for the carrier's next booking elsewhere in the network.
Delays anywhere in the documentation or clearance chain described earlier in this guide are consequently one of the
most common causes of demurrage specifically, since a shipment held up in customs for missing or inconsistent
paperwork continues accruing demurrage for every day it sits in the terminal past its free period, entirely independent
of any dispute over the goods themselves.
11. Common Reasons a Shipment Is Held at Customs
The overwhelming majority of customs holds trace back to a small set of recurring causes rather than anything
unusual about the goods themselves. Each is elaborated briefly below, since understanding why a given reason

causes a hold makes it easier to recognize and avoid in future shipments.
•
Value discrepancies between documents — When the declared value on the bill of entry does not match the
commercial invoice, or the invoice and packing list disagree on quantity, customs has no reliable basis to assess
duty and will hold the shipment until the discrepancy is explained or corrected.
•
Incorrect or inconsistent HS classification — A classification that looks aggressive relative to similar past
shipments, or that changes from one shipment to the next for what is ostensibly the same product, is a common
trigger for manual review, as discussed in Section 7.
•
Missing or invalid certificate of origin — Where preferential duty treatment is being claimed under a trade
agreement, an origin certificate that is missing, expired, or issued in the wrong format for that specific agreement
will prevent the preferential rate from being applied and can hold the shipment until either a valid certificate is
produced or the importer agrees to pay the standard duty rate instead.
•
Incomplete or missing licenses and permits — Certain product categories (for example chemicals,
pharmaceuticals, or restricted technologies) require an import or export license beyond the standard customs
declaration, and a shipment missing that license will be held regardless of how accurate the rest of its paperwork
is.
•
Random or risk-based inspection — Even a shipment with flawless documentation can be selected for physical
inspection under the red-channel process described in Section 8, simply as part of ordinary risk-based or
randomized customs enforcement; this is not itself a sign of any error in the paperwork.
•
Outstanding duties, penalties, or bond issues — A shipment can be held for reasons unrelated to the goods on
paper at all, such as an importer having an unresolved duty payment or penalty on a prior shipment, or (for
bonded-warehouse movements) an issue with the underlying customs bond itself.
